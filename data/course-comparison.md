# NEDS Course Comparison: Similarities & Differences

A well-sourced comparison of all nine courses/qualifications, covering what each one is, who it's for, and how they relate to one another. These fall into four distinct groups — it's worth understanding the groups first, since courses within a group share a lot in common, while courses across groups are quite different in kind (a driving licence category is not the same *type* of thing as a medical assessment or a compliance course, even though NEDS offers all of them).

## Quick comparison table

| Course | What it is | Minimum age | Prerequisite | Renewal / validity | Governing body |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| HGV Category C1 | Driving licence category | 18 | Full car (B) licence | Lifetime (renews with photocard, generally to age 45 then every 5 years like any licence) | DVLA / DVSA |
| HGV Category C | Driving licence category | 21 (some exceptions) | Full car (B) licence | Lifetime, as above | DVLA / DVSA |
| HGV Category C+E | Driving licence category | 21 | Full car (B) licence — Category C is not legally required first, though it's the common practical route | Lifetime, as above | DVLA / DVSA |
| Category B+E | Driving licence category | 17 (car licence minimum) | Full car (B) licence | Lifetime, as above | DVLA / DVSA |
| Driver CPC periodic training | Ongoing professional competence requirement | N/A (tied to holding a relevant HGV/PCV licence) | Must already hold Driver CPC (gained via initial qualification) | 35 hours every 5 years | DVSA |
| ADR (dangerous goods) | Specialist hazard-handling certificate | N/A (tied to relevant driving licence) | Relevant driving licence for the vehicle used | 5 years | Regulated under the ADR agreement (DVSA-linked) |
| Forklift truck training | Workplace competence certificate (not a driving licence) | 16 (counterbalance); 18 for some specialist trucks | None formally, though basic literacy/numeracy expected | 3–5 years (typically 5), refresher recommended after 6 months off the equipment | Accrediting bodies — RTITB, ITSSAR, or AITT (not DVLA) |
| Driver medical (D4) | Medical assessment, not a qualification | N/A | None — often the first step before applying for an LGV/PCV licence | Every 5 years age 45–65, annually after 65 | DVLA (form D4, alongside form D2) |

## Group 1: HGV and trailer driving licence categories (C1, C, C+E, B+E)

**Similarities:** All four are DVLA-issued driving licence categories, gained through a combination of theory and practical tests, and all require the person to already hold a full car (Category B) licence first. All are permanent entitlements added to a driving licence (not something that expires and needs re-earning, unlike the training/certificate courses below).

**Differences:** They're distinguished mainly by vehicle weight and whether a trailer is involved.
- **C1** covers rigid vehicles between 3.5 and 7.5 tonnes, and can be taken from age 18 — the youngest entry point into HGV driving.
- **C** covers rigid vehicles over 7.5 tonnes (often called Class 2), with a minimum age of 21 in most cases.
- **C+E** adds articulated or draw-bar trailer combinations to a Category C vehicle, up to 44 tonnes gross combination weight. Interestingly, current DVSA rules don't legally require holding Category C first — a new driver can go straight for C+E, and passing it awards C and C1 automatically — but going through C first is the more common and generally easier practical route, which is likely why NEDS's own course info frames C+E as being "for those who already hold a C licence."
- **B+E** is a different track entirely — it's for towing a trailer behind an ordinary car, with a combined maximum weight of 7 tonnes, and doesn't require any HGV entitlement at all.

## Group 2: Ongoing professional competence requirements (Driver CPC periodic training, ADR)

**Similarities:** Both are recurring legal training obligations for people who are already qualified drivers, rather than one-off licence categories. Both carry real consequences for lapsing — driving professionally without valid Driver CPC can mean fines of up to £1,000, and an expired ADR certificate means a driver can no longer legally carry the dangerous goods it covers. Both are renewed through further training and, in ADR's case, examination — not simply retaking a driving test.

**Differences:** Driver CPC is broad and applies to all professional lorry, bus, and coach drivers — 35 hours of periodic training every 5 years, covering general professional competence (safety, regulations, efficient driving, and so on). ADR is narrower and only relevant to drivers who carry dangerous/hazardous goods specifically — it renews every 5 years via refresher training and exams, and is only needed on top of a normal HGV licence if the job involves hazardous cargo.

## Group 3: Workplace and operational courses, not DVLA licences (Forklift training, OLAT)

**Similarities:** Neither of these produces a DVLA driving licence entitlement — they sit outside the DVLA/DVSA licensing system entirely, governed instead by workplace safety law (HSE) or industry training bodies. Both are aimed at competence and legal compliance in a work context rather than at "driving on the public road."

**Differences:** Forklift training is a hands-on operating qualification for the person actually driving the forklift, accredited by bodies like RTITB, ITSSAR, or AITT, with a minimum age as young as 16 for counterbalance trucks and renewal every 3–5 years. OLAT is the opposite end of the spectrum — it's a short (around 3-hour), largely theoretical online course aimed at operators, transport managers, and compliance staff who are *responsible for* commercial vehicle operations, not people who drive for a living. It covers legal obligations like operator licensing, drivers' hours, and vehicle maintenance duties, and has no minimum age or DVLA link at all.

## Group 4: Driver medical (D4)

The D4 medical stands apart from all the others — it isn't a qualification or a course in the training sense, but a medical fitness assessment carried out by a registered medical practitioner. It's typically the first step someone takes before applying for a provisional LGV or PCV licence (submitted to the DVLA alongside form D2), checking things like vision, blood pressure, diabetes management, and cardiovascular and neurological health. It then recurs — every 5 years between ages 45 and 65, and annually from 65 onward — for as long as someone holds an HGV or PCV entitlement. In that sense it's a prerequisite and ongoing condition for holding Group 1's licences, rather than a course someone completes once and is done with.

## Overall takeaway

Of the nine, four are permanent DVLA licence categories (C1, C, C+E, B+E), two are recurring legal training obligations tied to already holding a licence (Driver CPC, ADR), two are workplace competence/compliance courses entirely outside the DVLA system (forklift training, OLAT), and one is a recurring medical fitness check that underpins the others (D4). Someone building a full HGV driving career would typically move through several of these together — for example, a car licence, then C1 or C, a D4 medical to support the application, Driver CPC to keep driving professionally, and possibly ADR or forklift training depending on the specific job.
